Four words that describe every diamond.

The 4Cs are the universal way to describe a diamond's quality. Each one changes how a diamond looks and what it costs, and no single C tells the whole story. Here is what each one means, in plain words.

The first C

Color.

In a white diamond, color really means the lack of it. A chemically pure, structurally perfect diamond has no hue at all, like a drop of clear water, and that rarity makes it more valuable.

Diamonds are graded on a scale from D, colorless, to Z, a light yellow or brown, by comparing them to master stones under controlled light. Many neighboring grades look the same to an untrained eye, but they make a real difference in price.

Good to know: color is judged with the diamond upside down on white paper, not from the top.

The second C

Clarity.

Diamonds form under enormous heat and pressure deep in the earth. Most come out with tiny features inside, called inclusions, and marks on the surface, called blemishes.

Clarity grades how many there are, how big they are, and how easy they are to see at 10x magnification. There are 11 grades in six groups, from flawless to included. Most of the differences can't be seen without a loupe, which is why it pays to look at a stone with someone who can show you.

The third C

Cut.

Cut isn't the shape, like round, oval or pear. It's how well the diamond's facets handle light, and it's the C that most affects the sparkle.

A well-cut diamond shows three things: brightness, the white light reflected back to you; fire, flashes of rainbow color; and scintillation, the sparkle and pattern of light and dark as it moves. For round brilliants, cut is graded from Excellent to Poor, based on proportions, symmetry and polish.

The fourth C

Carat.

Carat is weight, not size. One carat is 200 milligrams, divided into 100 points, so a quarter-carat diamond is called a twenty-five pointer. Above one carat, weights are said in carats and decimals: a 1.08 carat stone is one point oh eight carats.

Two diamonds of the same weight can look different sizes, depending on how they're cut, which is why carat should be weighed alongside the other three Cs.
